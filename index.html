Artifact 1

80 Years of AI: A Timeline for Business Leaders

Adapted from AIML-500 Workshop 1 (AI & ML Timelines) · Audience: operations and small-business decision-makers
Introduction
Every few years a new wave of AI arrives with big promises. Business leaders have to decide whether to invest now, wait, or ignore the hype. This timeline shows how we got here and what history says about separating real progress from hype.
Description
A condensed timeline of AI and machine learning from 1943 to 2025. It covers the major breakthroughs, the two "AI winters," and the conditions that finally made modern AI work, and it ends with practical takeaways for operations leaders.
1943 – 1956 · Foundations

The idea of a thinking machine

McCulloch and Pitts model an artificial neuron (1943). Turing asks whether machines can think (1950). The Dartmouth Workshop names the field "artificial intelligence" (1956).

1958 – 1973 · Early optimism

The Perceptron and big promises

Rosenblatt's Perceptron learns simple patterns. Researchers predict human-level AI within a generation.

1974 – 1980 · First AI winter

Promises outrun the hardware

Results fall short, computers are too weak, and funding dries up.

1980 – 1987 · Expert systems

Rules written by hand

Companies build rule-based systems that capture expert knowledge. Backpropagation is popularized in 1986, but no one has the data or computing power to use it at scale.

1987 – 1993 · Second AI winter

Expensive and brittle

Expert systems prove costly to maintain and break outside their narrow rules.

1990s – 2000s · Statistical ML

Learning from data

Methods such as support vector machines and decision trees learn patterns from data instead of hand-written rules. Deep Blue beats Kasparov (1997).

2006 – 2016 · Deep learning & cloud

The missing pieces arrive

AWS launches cloud computing (2006), NVIDIA CUDA makes GPUs programmable (2007), and ImageNet provides a huge labeled dataset (2009). AlexNet (2012) proves deep learning works. AlphaGo beats a Go champion (2016).

2017 – 2025 · Generative AI

Transformers, chatbots, and agents

The Transformer architecture (2017) leads to large language models, ChatGPT (2022), and now reasoning models and AI agents. Today's key players include OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta, the cloud providers, and NVIDIA.

The big lesson: theory usually comes long before practice. Backpropagation existed in 1986, but deep learning didn't take off until 2012, when GPUs, cloud computing, and large datasets finally arrived together. Both AI winters happened because promises came before the infrastructure. That's a useful filter for today's hype: ask whether the data, tools, and people are actually in place.
Objective
To give non-technical decision-makers a short, accurate history of AI that helps them judge new AI claims and investments more realistically.
Process
I researched milestones from the course materials, grouped them into eras, and checked the dates against IEEE and Our World in Data resources. For this portfolio version I cut technical detail, added the "winters" as warning signs, and wrote the takeaways for an operations audience.
Tools and Technologies Used
Course readings, IEEE and Our World in Data sources, Claude (Anthropic) for organizing research and drafting the layout, and HTML/CSS for this page.
Value Proposition
Leaders get a two-minute way to put today's AI wave in context and a simple test for any AI pitch: are the data, computing power, and people ready for it?
Unique Value
Most AI timelines are written for technologists. This one is written from the operations side by someone who works with inventory data every day and also sells AI to small businesses.
Relevance
In supply chain the biggest current impacts are in demand forecasting and predictive maintenance, both built on the statistical-ML and deep-learning eras shown above. Knowing that history helps teams choose proven tools over hype.

Which AI Does Your Problem Need? Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

Adapted from AIML-500 Workshop 2 (2.3 Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning) · Audience: operations and small-business decision-makers
Introduction
"Use AI" isn't a plan. The first real question is what kind of AI fits the problem. Picking deep learning when a simple model would do wastes money, and picking a simple model for images or speech won't work at all.
Description
A one-page decision guide based on my course report comparing two cases: predicting which customers will cancel (traditional machine learning) and recognizing problems in images (deep learning).
Customer churn predictionImage recognition
Best approachTraditional ML (e.g., support vector machine)Deep learning (convolutional neural network)
DataRows and columns: tenure, monthly charges, support calls, contract typeRaw pixels from photos or scans
Who picks the features?People. The business already knows what matters.The model. The patterns are too subtle to define by hand.
Data neededThousands of recordsTens of thousands to millions of labeled images
ExplainabilityHigh. You can see why a customer was flagged.Lower. It needs extra tools to explain.
Real exampleTelecom and subscription churn models (Vafeiadis et al., 2015)Diabetic retinopathy screening that matched eye specialists (Gulshan et al., 2016)
Why not the other?Deep learning would cost more, need more data, and overfit small tables.Hand-built features break when lighting or cameras change and miss subtle signs.
Rule of thumb: if your data already fits in a spreadsheet, start with traditional machine learning. If it's images, audio, or free text, you'll probably need deep learning, along with the data and computing power to support it.
Objective
To help non-technical leaders choose the right type of AI before they spend money, by focusing on the shape of their data.
Process
I chose one traditional ML example and one deep learning example from the course lesson, found a published real-world application for each, and explained why each approach fits and why the other doesn't. For the portfolio I turned the written report into a side-by-side comparison and a rule of thumb.
Tools and Technologies Used
AIML-500 lesson content, published research (JAMA; Simulation Modelling Practice and Theory), Claude (Anthropic) for structuring and drafting, and HTML/CSS.
Value Proposition
A business owner can look at this table and know in a minute whether a vendor's "deep learning" pitch makes sense for their problem.
Unique Value
The churn example comes straight from my own business: Vosudu sells a monthly service, so knowing which clients are at risk is a real problem I deal with, not a textbook exercise.
Relevance
Most operations and small-business data, such as orders, inventory, and customer accounts, is tabular. That means traditional ML is often the faster, cheaper, and more explainable starting point.

Neural Networks in 60 Seconds, and How to Get AI to Explain Anything Simply

Adapted from AIML-500 Workshop 3 (3.4 Communicating for Learning) · Audience: operations and small-business decision-makers
Introduction
Leaders are increasingly asked to approve, buy, or roll out tools built on neural networks, often without a clear picture of what one is. This artifact gives a one-minute plain-language explanation, plus the prompting technique I used to produce it, so readers can get the same kind of clear explanation for any technical topic.
Description
A short explainer built around an everyday analogy, followed by a repeatable four-step prompt recipe for turning generic AI output into an explanation a specific audience will actually remember.

What's a neural network? Think about how you learned to pick a ripe avocado.

At first, you probably checked one thing, like the color. But color alone fools you. Over time you learned to combine clues: dark skin and a slight give when squeezed and a stem cap that comes off easily. That combination means "ripe." A neural network learns the same way, just from thousands of examples instead of years of grocery trips.

PartWhat it doesAvocado version
InputsThe facts the model looks atColor, firmness, size, stem
Hidden layersCombine facts into patterns, from simple to complex"Pretty dark" → "dark + soft = probably ripe"
OutputThe model's answer, often as a probability"85% chance this one is ripe"
LearningAfter each wrong guess, adjusts how much it trusts each clueGets better with every avocado

The catch: it only knows what it was shown. Train it only on Hass avocados and it may misjudge a green Florida avocado. Good examples in, good answers out.

The prompt recipe: (1) Name the audience. (2) Ask for an analogy from their everyday world. (3) Ask for a short, scannable structure. (4) Ask it to state one honest limitation. A generic "explain X simply" prompt gets a correct but forgettable answer. These four additions are what make it stick.
Objective
To help non-technical decision-makers understand the basic mechanics of neural networks, and to give them a reusable method for getting clear explanations of any AI concept from generative AI tools.
Process
For the class discussion I used Claude (Anthropic) to create a simple explanation of neural networks, applying the course lesson's communication strategies: tailored approach, storytelling, clear structure, and honesty about limits. For the portfolio I turned the explanation into a reference table and added the prompt recipe, so the reader gets both the answer and the method.
Tools and Technologies Used
Claude (Anthropic) for iterative prompting and drafting, AIML-500 Workshop 3 lesson content on communication for learning, and HTML/CSS for this page.
Value Proposition
A leader can understand what a neural network does in one minute and can reuse the four-step recipe to get clear explanations of any technical topic before making a decision.
Unique Value
Most neural network explainers either drown readers in math or oversimplify with "it works like a brain." This one uses a concrete everyday analogy that maps directly onto the real components, and it teaches the communication technique behind it.
Relevance
Neural networks power many of the AI tools businesses are adopting, from demand forecasting to voice assistants and image inspection. Leaders who understand the basics, especially that a model is only as good as its examples, ask better questions before buying or deploying these tools.
